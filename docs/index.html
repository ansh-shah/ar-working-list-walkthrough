The AR Working List

One prioritized queue for every open claim we're owed money on. The AR team works it daily, the system measures the work on its own, and it has been running for about a year now. This is a walkthrough of how it works and a conversation about where it goes next.

1 · Why it exists

Our AR data lived in three places. Our priorities lived in nobody's.

Before the working list, an AR rep deciding "what do I work today?" had to reconcile all of this by hand:

CentralReachWhat we billed, what was paid, dates of service
SalesforceWhich patient, which center and region, account status
Denials & rejectionsWhy the payer didn't pay: codes, reasons, dates, plus Office Ally rejections
→
One claim-level working listjoined, categorized, and sorted in the priority order we actually care about

No shared priority

Which matters more, an aged denial or a big underpayment? Every rep answered differently.

No single view

A claim's billing, its denial reason, and its patient context never sat on the same row.

No measurement

We couldn't say what got worked, when, or whether the work actually moved dollars.

3 · The prioritized queue

Every claim gets a priority number. Reps work top-down.

The category encodes what we care about: denials before silence, oldest first, then underpayments and cleanup queues. Reps don't decide what matters most; the list already did.

The priority ladder

1
Unpaid 91+, denied. The oldest denied claims. Worked first, every day.
2
Unpaid 61-90, denied. Denials next in line as they age.
3
Unpaid 60 or less, denied. Fresh denials, caught before they age.
4
Unpaid 91+, no response. The payer went silent months ago.
5
Unpaid 61-90, no response. Silence that's starting to age.
6
Unpaid 60 or less, no response. The bulk of the book. Most of these pay on their own without a touch.
7
Underpayment. Paid, but under the agreed rate.
8+
Cleanup queues. Credits, negative payments, and not-yet-billed, each with their own playbook.
Denials outrank silence, and older outranks newer. A rep opens their tab and simply works from the top.

5 · What comes out the other side

Work measured in dollars, not anecdotes

Because every touch and every dollar delta is captured, the reporting layer can answer questions we simply couldn't before:

Per rep

Claims assigned · touches this month · follow-ups completed · $ collected per follow-up

Per work type

Denials reworked, payer projects, rebills, each with touches and average $ moved per touch

Per payer

Where the money is stuck, and whether it's a claim problem or a systemic payer project (e.g. one large payer reconciliation worked as a single project instead of hundreds of touches)

Per denial category

Authorization, coverage, credentialing: what's driving non-payment and how fast each category resolves

Questions we can answer now that we couldn't before

Which denial categories are aging toward write-off, and at which payers?
Did last month's follow-ups actually move money, and whose did?
Is that payer problem systemic, or a pile of unrelated claims?
How does our internal team compare to Equalize on the claims we each work?
Which root causes keep coming back and belong upstream, not in AR?
Where should next month's rep hours actually go?

A handful of payers hold most of the outstanding balance, which is why payer-level projects exist alongside claim-by-claim work. These are the questions Raymond's bad-debt and effectiveness goals turn into; the future-state section picks them up.

6 · Where this goes next

A year in, the machine works. Now it can start steering.

1

The bad-debt lens: reporting our way to 3.5%

Goal-direct
Bad debt is AR we never managed to collect. This tool sees every dollar of AR before it gets there, already tagged with the reason it's stuck, which makes it a natural engine for new reporting:
  • Bad-debt bridge: trace eventual write-offs back to their denial categories, so "we missed 3.5%" becomes "authorization denials in two payers drove X%."
  • Early-warning views: claims aging toward write-off risk, flagged while they're still workable.
  • Prevention loop: recurring root causes routed upstream (credentialing, authorizations, billing setup) instead of being re-worked claim by claim forever.
Why it matters
The denial categories, aging, and outcomes are already captured. This is largely a reporting build, not a data build.
2

One yardstick: our team vs. Equalize RCM

Effectiveness
Equalize RCM (sometimes written "ERCM") works a share of our claims, but they don't work inside the Google Sheet, so we can't see their activity at the same level of detail as our own reps — only the claims they tell us they've worked. What we can do is measure their back-end effectiveness: track the claims we assign them and follow the payment data on those claims, side by side with the claims our team works.
  • Scott and Eric have been building views to describe the internal/outsourced breakout. This tool can supply the observed numbers underneath those views.
  • Current effectiveness calculations aren't fully fleshed out; assignment plus payment data gives us a defensible, automatic basis for the comparison, even without Equalize's touch-level activity.
Why it matters
Whether we grow the internal team, expand outsourcing, or rebalance, that decision deserves a measured answer, not an impression.
3

Fresh data: monthly today, weekly next, then daily

BigQuery
Today the list rebuilds monthly, so by week three the team is working from aging information. Equalize gets comparable results partly because they run on a substantially better data environment. Fresher data is a real advantage, and right now our vendor has better plumbing than our own team does.
  • The company is moving its legacy SQL warehouse to BigQuery, and CentralReach is being connected directly to it. Ansh has been driving that build with IT.
  • Once that lands, the monthly build becomes a scheduled job over live data: weekly refresh is straightforward, daily is realistic.
  • Fresher data compounds everything else on this list: priorities, follow-ups, and reporting all improve with it.
Why it matters
The biggest force multiplier on this list, and the enabling work is already in motion.
4

Reporting cadence tied to KPIs and goals

Steering
Today's reporting answers "what happened." A regular cadence of weekly or monthly scorecards with agreed KPIs turns the tool from a rear-view mirror into a steering wheel:
  • Set outcome expectations (aged-AR %, resolve rates, $/follow-up) and let the list's priorities be tuned to match them.
  • Standing artifacts for finance leadership rather than ad hoc pulls.
Why it matters
The tool already changes behavior daily at the rep level; cadence lets it do the same at the management level.
5

From Google Sheet to a real product

UI / UX
The reps have effectively co-designed their ideal workflow inside the sheet: restricted dropdowns, auto follow-up dates, per-rep views. But a spreadsheet has ceilings around concurrency, speed at 100k rows, guardrails, and integration.
  • The workflow is proven and documented, which is exactly where you want to be before building software.
  • Options: an internal IT build on the new BigQuery foundation, or an external vendor. Worth scoping both.
Why it matters
A year of daily use is the best requirements document we'll ever have.
